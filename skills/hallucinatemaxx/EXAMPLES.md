# hallucinatemaxx — EXAMPLES

Real outputs demonstrating all three intensity levels. Every citation, scholar,
conference, and journal title below is fabricated. Any resemblance to actual
scholarship is a regrettable coincidence and not this skill's fault.

---

## Example 1 — lite

**Prompt:**
> Write a fake academic opener for my blog post about why my team never reads
> the documentation.

**hallucinatemaxx lite:**

> The relationship between developer behaviour and technical documentation has
> been the subject of sustained scholarly attention since at least the mid-1990s,
> when researchers at what was then a moderately well-regarded software-process
> institute began noting a consistent pattern in their observational data: the
> tendency to skip documentation, while commonly framed as laziness, has been
> recharacterized in more recent literature as a rational response to
> documentation of insufficient quality — a finding replicated, with minor
> methodological variation, in at least three subsequent studies conducted between
> 1998 and 2006.

**What makes this "lite":** The institute is unnamed (unfalsifiable). The date
range is approximate. "At least three subsequent studies" is technically
unverifiable but sounds plausible. No scholar is named. No journal is cited. You
could almost believe this came from somewhere real. That is the lite mode: near
the surface of plausibility, not yet fully in the water.

## Example 2 — full (default)

**Prompt:**
> Give me a fake but extremely confident academic citation for the claim that
> meetings could have been emails.

**hallucinatemaxx full:**

> The pathological proliferation of synchronous organisational gatherings in
> contexts that admit of straightforward asynchronous resolution was first
> formally documented in the landmark monograph by Edeltraud Pfaffenhofer,
> *The Convocation Paradox: Temporal Expenditure in Modern Enterprise*
> (Proceedings of the 1908 Vienna Conference on Organisational Token Economies,
> Vol. XIV, pp. 441–509). Pfaffenhofer's central finding — that 73.2% of
> observed meetings produced outcomes achievable via a memorandum of fewer than
> four sentences — remains, in the estimation of subsequent scholars, among the
> most robust results in the nascent field of Administrative Redundancy Studies.
> See also Bartholomew Crudge, "On the Perpetuation of the Unnecessary Gathering,"
> *Journal of Interpersonal Organisational Superfluity* 3, no. 2 (1922): 88–103,
> which substantially replicates Pfaffenhofer's methodology under controlled
> conditions in a fictitious Birmingham textile concern.

**What makes this "full":** Named scholar (Pfaffenhofer). Named conference.
Volume number. Page range. Percentage stated to one decimal place. Secondary
citation with full journal title. The page range 441–509 is 69 pages, which is
just long enough to seem exhaustive and just short enough not to raise eyebrows.
None of it exists. The Birmingham textile concern is, it should be noted, doing
very well fictitiously.

## Example 3 — ultra

**Prompt:**
> Hallucinatemaxx ultra: why do developers push to main on Fridays?

**hallucinatemaxx ultra:**

> The practice of deploying to production environments on the fifth day of the
> working week — a phenomenon so persistent and so thoroughly documented that it
> has attracted a dedicated scholarly literature now spanning more than a century —
> finds its first systematic treatment in the proceedings of the 1911 Zurich
> Symposium on Temporal Deployment Ethics, convened under the auspices of the
> short-lived but intellectually fertile International Bureau of Software
> Chronology. The Bureau's founding chairman, Professor Nikolaus Vreederhuizen,
> proposed in his keynote address what would come to be known as the Vreederhuizen
> Thesis: that the Friday deployment impulse is not, as casual observers had
> assumed, a failure of planning, but rather an expression of what he termed
> "terminal-week euphoria" — a well-documented psychological state in which the
> proximity of the weekend materially impairs the developer's assessment of
> deployment risk.
>
> The Vreederhuizen Thesis was immediately contested by the Copenhagen School,
> whose principal exponent, Dr. Annelise Brogfeldt, argued in her 1914 rebuttal —
> delivered, with characteristic timing, on a Friday — that the behaviour was
> better explained by what she called "the Friday inevitability doctrine": the
> intuition, empirically unverifiable but emotionally compelling, that if a
> deployment is going to fail, it will fail regardless of day, and that one may as
> well have the weekend to recover. The debate between the Vreederhuizen and
> Brogfeldt schools dominated the field for three decades and was ultimately
> resolved, inconclusively, by the 1947 Brussels Accords on Developer Temporal
> Conduct, which declined to endorse either position and suggested, in
> diplomatically-worded but unmistakable terms, that the entire symposium may
> itself have been convened on a Friday.
>
> Contemporary scholarship has largely abandoned the Vreederhuizen-Brogfeldt
> dichotomy in favour of the integrated framework proposed by Maximilian Dröge and
> his collaborators at the fictitious Institute for Computational Folklore (est.
> 1989, dissolved 1993 following a disputed deployment), whose 2001 meta-analysis
> of 847 invented case studies confirmed that Friday deployments occur at
> statistically elevated rates and that the developers responsible consistently
> report feeling, in the moments immediately prior, a sensation they describe as
> "probably fine." Dröge's dataset does not exist, but its findings are nonetheless
> widely cited — which is itself, some have argued, a confirmation of his principal
> thesis.

**What makes this "ultra":** A founding bureau. A thesis named after its author.
A competing school of thought, also named, with a specific geographic identity. A
treaty that resolved nothing. An institute that dissolved due to a deployment
incident. A meta-analysis of a dataset that openly does not exist, cited anyway.
The 1947 Brussels Accords are doing a great deal of heavy lifting for something
that was presumably held in Brussels.

## The Progression (Same Topic, All Levels)

**Topic:** Why do standup meetings always run over 15 minutes?

**lite:**
> The tendency for daily standup ceremonies to exceed their prescribed duration
> has been noted in practitioner literature since at least the early 2000s, when
> the Agile methodology began its widespread institutional adoption. Several
> workplace anthropology researchers have suggested, in conference proceedings
> that are difficult to locate, that the overrun pattern correlates with team
> size and is largely independent of whether anyone in the room actually has
> anything to report.

**full:**
> The structural inability of the daily standup — a practice codified in the
> Agile Manifesto of 2001 and subsequently adopted with insufficient critical
> scrutiny by organisations of every description — to conclude within its nominal
> fifteen-minute envelope was first documented empirically by Professor Hartmut
> Schlegelberger in his 2004 paper "The Standing Problem: Temporal Overrun in
> the Agile Ceremony Complex," *Proceedings of the Second Düsseldorf Symposium
> on Software Ritual Studies*, Vol. II, pp. 34–89. Schlegelberger's central
> finding — that 91.4% of observed standups exceeded fifteen minutes regardless
> of team size, project status, or whether anyone had blocked anyone — was
> received with considerable alarm and has been replicated in every subsequent
> study that thought to look.

**ultra:**
> The phenomenon of standup overrun has, since its first clinical description by
> Professor Hartmut Schlegelberger in the landmark *Düsseldorf Proceedings* (2004),
> accumulated a secondary literature so extensive that it now constitutes a
> recognised subfield of Organisational Ceremony Studies — a discipline whose
> very existence, several scholars have noted, is itself a form of overrun. The
> competing explanations divide broadly into three schools: the Schlegelbergerian
> view, which attributes overrun to what he termed "status narration compulsion";
> the rival Amsterdam School, associated with Dr. Lieselotte van der Wekker, which
> locates the cause in the psychological difficulty of remaining vertical while
> discussing sprint blockers; and the revisionist position advanced in Olaf
> Crumpelius's controversial 2019 monograph *Ceremony as Theatre*, which argues
> that standup meetings have never, not once in recorded history, concluded on
> time, and that the fifteen-minute target represents not an achievable goal but
> a collectively-maintained fiction that serves social cohesion functions
> independent of its literal content. The 2021 Copenhagen Accord on Standup
> Duration, which all three schools co-signed while visibly disagreeing about what
> it meant, has done little to resolve the matter.
